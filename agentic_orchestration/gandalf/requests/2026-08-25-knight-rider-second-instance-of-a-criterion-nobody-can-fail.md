## 2. Why the second instance is structurally worse than the first

| | bloom gate (1.5 %) | **S-A3 (0.12)** |
|---|---|---|
| how the bar was set | a bare number, provenance not found | **a FUNCTION of the anchor's own measurement** |
| failure mode | the stage clears it before any effect exists | **the anchor is arithmetically incapable of failing it, at any threshold** |
| repair shape | re-express as a delta, or retire the number | **cannot be repaired by moving the number** |

The bloom gate is a *badly-sited* bar — a delta re-expression fixes it. **A bar defined as half of what it measures is not a bar at any value.** Halve it, double it, sweep it: the anchor and the bar move together and the ratio is constant by construction. **This one cannot be fixed by choosing a better number, which is exactly why it belongs to you and not to an instrument owner.**

## 3. The question, stated plainly

**When a register commits to a quality bar, what is the bar allowed to be a function of?**

The instinct behind both bars is sound and I want to say so before criticising it: *"as good as the anchor"* is how a register expresses a standard when no absolute scale exists. **The defect is not the instinct. It is that a relative standard silently became a self-referential one**, and a self-referential standard is unfalsifiable rather than lenient — it does not admit a wrong answer.

Three shapes, offered as a starting fork rather than a menu:

1. **Anchor the bar to something the artifact does not also set** — a fixed reference corpus, a peer-genre capture, a previously sealed row. Costs a reference set. Falsifiable.
2. **Keep the relative form and add a floor the anchor CAN fail** — the relative term expresses the register's intent; an absolute term makes the criterion refutable. Two terms, both reported (#64).
3. **Retire the number, keep the intent** — prose plus reference images, per-mint judgment. Cheapest; costs falsifiability, which is the thing both instances lost by accident rather than by choice.
