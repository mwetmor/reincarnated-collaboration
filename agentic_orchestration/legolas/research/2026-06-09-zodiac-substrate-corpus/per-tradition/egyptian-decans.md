## Tradition overview

The Egyptian decans are 36 star-groups used in ancient Egyptian astronomy to divide the ecliptic into 36 sectors of 10° each. They originated in the First Intermediate Period of Egypt (coffin lid appearances from ~2100 BCE) with roots in the Old Kingdom (~2400 BCE), and represent one of humanity's earliest systematic astronomical frameworks.

**Core functions:**
1. **Star-clock:** Each decan rose heliacally (just before dawn) to mark a 10-day period (dekad) of the 360-day Egyptian calendar. 36 decans × 10 days = 360 days, plus 5 epagomenal festival days.
2. **Hour-marker:** At night, the sequential rising of decans across the eastern horizon marked the passage of nighttime hours — effectively a precision sky-clock.
3. **Coffin-table calendar:** Middle Kingdom coffin lids (~2000 BCE) displayed "diagonal star tables" showing which decans rose at each hour of each dekad — the deceased king needed this knowledge to navigate the underworld night sky.
4. **Dendera synthesis:** The Ptolemaic Dendera Zodiac (~50 BCE, now in the Louvre) shows all 36 decans as divine figures in procession, combined with the Babylonian/Greek 12-sign zodiac — a fusion of Egyptian and Hellenistic astronomical traditions.

**Key scholarly note (Sarah Symons, cited in Wikipedia):** Although the decan names are documented, "the locations of the decanal stars and their relationships to modern star names and constellations are not known" with certainty. The exact star correspondences remain contested in academic Egyptology.

## Sequencing note

The Wikipedia article on Decan presents the 36 decans in two mappings:
- **Ancient Egyptian sequence** (positions 1-36): Begins with Sirius/Sopdet (position 1) in what maps to Cancer in the Western zodiac; ends at position 36 with A-Saḥ (Orion) in Gemini.
- **Western zodiac ordering** (Aries through Pisces): 3 decans per zodiac sign; this is the Greco-Roman Ptolemaic ordering.

The corpus entries use the Western zodiac ordering for `position_in_system` (1-36, Aries to Pisces) and record `ancient_sequence_position` for cross-reference.

## Key cross-tradition parallels

The Egyptian decan system is the **origin tradition** for a cascade of derivative astronomical systems:

1. **Greek/Hellenistic:** Decans adopted in Ptolemy's *Tetrabiblos*; each decan acquired a planetary ruler. The Greco-Egyptian synthesis visible in the Dendera zodiac.
2. **Hermetic tradition:** Greek Hermeticism assigned demon names to each decan (preserved in corpus as `testament_of_solomon_demon` and Hermetic deity associations). The Hermetic writings of Cosmas of Maiuma, Julius Firmicus Maternus, Scaliger, and Kircher all catalog decan figures.
3. **Jewish/Christian demonology:** The *Testament of Solomon* (likely 1st-5th century CE) reinterpreted the 36 decans as 36 demons — one of the most striking examples of astronomical figures being demonized across religious traditions. Note: Decan 27 (Kenmu) received the name "Satan" in this tradition.
4. **Indian:** Imported as *drekkāṇa* (dreshkana/drikana) — the 36 10° divisions of the zodiac. Mentioned by Sphujidhvaja (Yavanajataka, ~269 CE) and Varahamihira (Brihat-Samhita, ~550 CE). Modern scholars confirm Indian transmission via Greece from Egypt.
5. **Arabic Manazil connection:** Wikipedia's Decan article explicitly notes that "decans gave way to a lunar division of 27 or 28 lunar stations, also known as manzil (Arabic: منزل)." The 28 Manazil and the 36 decans are historically linked as successive astronomical frameworks.
6. **Japanese:** Decan figures appear in Japanese astrology from Kamakura through Edo periods, possibly via Chinese or Indian transmission.

## Orion/Osiris body-map observation

A notable pattern in decans 6-9 (Taurus 20° through Gemini 30°): four consecutive decans carry names referencing Orion/Saḥ body parts:
- Decan 6: "Shoulder of Orion" (Remen-ḥeru-an-Saḥ)
- Decan 7: "Forearm of Orion" (Mestcher-Saḥ)
- Decan 8: "Lower arm of Orion" (Remen-kher-Saḥ)
- Decan 9: "Of Orion" (A-Saḥ)

This suggests the ancient Egyptians systematically mapped Orion (Saḥ = Osiris's celestial form) onto this region of the sky using decan nomenclature — effectively annotating Osiris's body in the stars.
