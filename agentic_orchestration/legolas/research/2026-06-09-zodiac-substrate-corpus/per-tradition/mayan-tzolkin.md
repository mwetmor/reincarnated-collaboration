## Tradition overview

The Tzolkin (Tz'olk'in in modern K'iche' orthography) is a 260-day sacred divinatory calendar formed by 20 named day-signs cycling through 13 numbers (1–13), producing 260 unique day-name combinations before the cycle repeats. It originated in the Pre-Classic Maya period (~2000 BCE) and was codified in its canonical form during the Classic period (~250–900 CE) as documented in surviving Mayan codices including the Dresden Codex. Unlike solar or lunar calendars, the 260-day count has no direct astronomical correlate — its probable origin relates to the human gestation period (~266 days) and the agricultural growing season in the Maya highlands. The Tzolkin remains a living ceremonial tradition actively practiced by K'iche', Kaqchikel, Tz'utujil, and Mam Maya communities in highland Guatemala, where day-keepers (ajq'ij) consult the calendar for naming ceremonies, planting timing, marriage compatibility, and ritual healing.

## Mayan-Aztec parallel structure

The Tzolkin and Tonalpohualli are cognate systems — both 20 day-signs × 13 numbers = 260 days. The day-signs align in position (1=1, 2=2, etc.) though the names, animal associations, and presiding deities differ. The parallel likely reflects a shared Proto-Mesoamerican predecessor calendar tradition that diversified across cultures.

## Gaps + Phase 2 notes

**Gaps identified:**

1. **Stellar coordinates absent by design** — The Tzolkin is a calendar-based, not stellar, system. Day-signs are not assigned to constellations. The Pleiades heliacal rising was important for Maya calendar reckoning but is not assigned to individual day-signs. This gap is structural, not a research failure. If substrate needs a celestial hook, Lamat (Venus) is the exception.

2. **Codex imagery not fully resolved** — The Dresden Codex contains day-sign imagery but specific page citations for each sign were not individually verified. Phase 2 could map each day-sign to specific Dresden Codex pages for higher-fidelity visual sourcing.

3. **Regional variant traditions** — The 20 day-signs have regional naming variants across the 30+ living Maya languages. This corpus documents Yucatec and K'iche' only. Phase 2 could add Kaqchikel, Tz'utujil, and Mam variants.

4. **Ceremonial calendar still in active use** — The K'iche' and Kaqchikel communities in highland Guatemala continue to use the Tzolkin for ceremony. Phase 2 should consult Tedlock (1982) more deeply and potentially FAMSI (Foundation for the Advancement of Mesoamerican Studies) resources for ceremonial application detail.

5. **Image URL verification** — Wikipedia Commons glyph PNG paths follow the MAYA-g-log-cal-D[NN]-[Name].png pattern. All URLs are format-verified against the Wikipedia Tzolk'in article's glyph series; live-load verification recommended before production use.

**Substrate application notes:**

- The 20-sign cycle with no stellar correlate makes Tzolkin signs strong candidates for **calendar/time-based** game mechanics rather than constellation-form characters
- K'iche' names tend to have richer semantic depth (Toj=payment, No'j=intelligence, B'atz'=thread) than Yucatec names — K'iche' vocabulary is more promising for game title/flavor text generation
- The Popol Vuh (Hero Twins / Hunahpu-Xbalanque narrative) is a deep mythic source that connects to multiple day-signs (Ajaw/Ajpu, Chuen/B'atz', Kimi) — strong candidate for kit narrative substrate
- Parallel with Aztec Tonalpohualli (same 20 positions) enables cross-cultural comparison analysis for substrate diversity metrics
