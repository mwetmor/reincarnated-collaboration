## Tradition overview

The Japanese Jūnishi (十二支, "Twelve Earthly Branches") is the Japanese adoption of the Chinese sexagenary zodiac system, transmitted to Japan during the Nara period (710–794 CE) as part of the broad Chinese cultural influence that reshaped Japanese court culture. The system governs traditional time-reckoning (years, months, hours) using the 12 Earthly Branches and is most visibly expressed in Japan through New Year (Oshōgatsu) culture: ema votive plaques at Shinto shrines, nengajō greeting cards, and seasonal decorative arts. Japan modified the 12th animal from Pig to Wild Boar (Inoshishi) to reflect native wildlife reality.

## Cultural differences from Chinese source

- **Position 12 — Wild Boar (Inoshishi) for Pig:** Japan's most significant substitution. Wild boars (猪, Inoshishi) are native to Japan and culturally significant; domestic pigs were less embedded. The Wild Boar symbolizes fierce courage and direct action.
- **Position 8 — Sheep (Hitsuji) specified:** Japan is explicit that this is Hitsuji (羊, domestic sheep) rather than yagi (goat), unlike the ambiguous Chinese 未 character that encompasses both.
- **Ema tradition:** The entire ema (絵馬) votive-plaque system — central to Japanese zodiac shrine practice — etymologically originates from the Horse sign (Uma). Ema literally means "picture horse."
- **New Year expression:** Japan celebrates New Year (Oshōgatsu) on January 1 (Gregorian) since 1873; the zodiac animal cycle remains symbolically active but the calendar itself is Gregorian.
- **Moon Rabbit:** Japan's Rabbit (position 4) connects strongly to the Moon Rabbit (Tsuki no Usagi) folkloric tradition — the rabbit seen in the moon making mochi rice cakes.
- **Benzaiten snake:** Japan's White Snake at position 6 (Mi/Hebi) is a sacred messenger of Benzaiten, goddess of water/music/wisdom — a specifically Japanese religious association.
- **Three wise monkeys:** Japan's native macaque at position 9 (Saru) is inseparable from the three wise monkeys (Mizaru, Kikazaru, Iwazaru) carved at Nikkō Tōshō-gū — global cultural export.
