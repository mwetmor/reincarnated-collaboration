## Tradition overview

The 27 Vedic Nakshatras (Sanskrit: नक्षत्र, from na + kshatra = "indestructible") are the lunar mansions of Hindu astronomical and astrological tradition (Jyotish), dividing the ecliptic into 27 equal segments of 13°20′ each — one for each day of the sidereal lunar month. The system's origins trace to the Vedanga Jyotisha (~1200-900 BCE) and the Rigveda, making it one of the oldest systematic stellar catalogues in the world; classical codification in the Parashara school (~200-400 CE) established the standard deity-symbol-gana-nadi-rashi framework still used today. Each Nakshatra is associated with a presiding deity (its metaphysical ruler), a ruling planet (its Jyotish graha), a gana (temperament class: Deva/divine, Manushya/human, or Rakshasa/fierce), a yogatara (primary determinative star), and a rich mythological narrative. Unlike the Western 12-sign zodiac (tropical, equal houses), the Nakshatra system is sidereal, constellation-based, and primarily structured around the Moon's movement rather than the Sun's — making it a uniquely lunar tradition with direct stellar anchor points for every entry.

**Astronomical notes of interest for substrate use:**

- Mula (19) points toward the Galactic Center — cosmologically extraordinary for a "root" nakshatra
- Revati (ζ Piscium) is literally the star that defines the traditional sidereal zero-point
- Abhijit (Vega) is a future pole star — precession narrative embedded in the mythology
- Four Royal Stars of Persia (Aldebaran, Regulus, Antares, Fomalhaut) — three appear as nakshatra yogatara (Rohini, Magha, Jyeshtha)
