## Tradition overview

The 28 Arabic Manazil al-Qamar (مَنَازِل الْقَمَر, "stations of the Moon") are the Islamic astronomical tradition's division of the ecliptic into 28 lunar mansions, each representing approximately one day's travel of the Moon through the sky (the Moon moves ~13°/day; 28 × 13° ≈ 364°).

**Core functions:**
1. **Lunar calendar:** The Moon's monthly position was tracked relative to the 28 Manazil. Each Manzil lasts approximately 13 days in the year-cycle (not one day — see note below).
2. **Weather prediction:** One or more Manazil were grouped into an *anwa* (plural: *anwaa*) associated with a dominant weather pattern. The anwaa system was the primary agricultural and meteorological calendar for Arab farmers and pastoralists.
3. **Navigation:** Arabic navigators used Manazil as sky-reference points for maritime and desert navigation.
4. **Linguistic legacy:** Many modern star names in Western astronomy are direct Arabic survivals from the Manazil tradition: Aldebaran (from Ad-Dabaran), Shaula (from Ash-Shawlah), Zubenelgenubi/Zubeneschamali (from Az-Zubana), Sadalsuud (from Sa'd as-Su'ud), Sadachbia (from Sa'd al-Akhbiyah), Albali (from Sa'd Bula), Mirach, Markab, Scheat, and others.

**Calendar note:** Per Wikipedia, each Manzil lasts approximately 13 days in the year-cycle (not 1 day). The Wikipedia table includes approximate "begins on" dates for each Manzil. The 28 Manazil do not perfectly divide the year; the table notes 2 days missing from a solar year.

**Key academic anchor:** Al-Sufi, *Kitab Suwar al-Kawakib al-Thabita* (Book of Fixed Stars), 964 CE, Abbasid Caliphate. Authored by Abd al-Rahman al-Sufi (Azophi), a Persian astronomer working in Arabic. The oldest surviving copy is the 1125 CE manuscript at the Museum of Islamic Art, Doha (MS 2.1998). The Bodleian Library holds a late 12th-century copy (MS Marsh 144).

## Key cross-tradition parallels

The 28 Manazil are one of three nearly-identical 27/28 lunar mansion systems found across Afro-Eurasian astronomy:

| Tradition | Count | Name | Region | Key Text |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Arabic / Islamic | 28 | Manazil al-Qamar | Arabian Peninsula / Islamic world | Al-Sufi, Book of Fixed Stars (964 CE) |
| Indian / Hindu | 27/28 | Nakshatras | Indian subcontinent | Vedanga Jyotisha (~1200 BCE?); Atharvaveda |
| Chinese | 28 | Xiu (宿) | East Asia | Various classical texts; linked to Four Symbols |
| Somali | 28 | God | Horn of Africa | Galaal, traditional weather-lore |

**The parallel is striking enough that it has generated scholarly debate** about common origin vs. independent invention. Wikipedia notes the concept may originate in Babylonian astronomy (6th century BCE); the 28 stations "scheme was derived via Egyptian magic by the linking of lists of lucky and unlucky days of the lunar month with the hemerologies and with the zodiac" (Tester 1987). The Arabic-Indian parallel is particularly close: many of the Manazil correspond directly to Nakshatras in both star content and order.

**Egyptian decan connection:** Wikipedia's Decan article explicitly states "decans gave way to a lunar division of 27 or 28 lunar stations, also known as manzil." The Manazil may represent a successor system to the Egyptian 36-decan framework — a 28-station lunar-month system replacing the 36-station solar-year system.

## Thematic patterns across the 28 Manazil

**The great Arabic lion (Manazil 7-12):** The most distinctive feature of Arabic celestial mythology. In Arabic tradition, a great lion stretched from Gemini through Leo:
- Manazil 7 (Castor/Pollux): lion's extended forearms
- Manazil 8 (Praesepe/Cancer): lion's nose
- Manazil 9 (Leo/Cancer border): lion's eyes
- Manazil 10 (Regulus): lion's forehead
- Manazil 11 (δ/θ Leo): lion's mane
- Manazil 12 (Denebola): lion's tail

This gives the Arabic celestial lion the longest stellar body of any zodiacal figure — spanning four modern constellations.

**The Sa'd fortune series (Manazil 22-25):** Four consecutive "lucky" Manazil in Capricorn/Aquarius:
- Sa'd adh-Dhabih (22): fortune of sacrifice
- Sa'd Bula (23): voracious fortune
- Sa'd as-Su'ud (24): luckiest of the lucky — the most auspicious Manzil in the system
- Sa'd al-Akhbiyah (25): fortune of tents

**The water-bucket pair (Manazil 26-27):** The Great Square of Pegasus stars reimagined as the two spouts of a water bucket — connecting to the water theme of the preceding Aquarius Sa'd Manazil.

**The scorpion sequence (Manazil 16-19):** The scorpion is presented in body segments:
- Claws (Libra stars, 16)
- Crown (β/δ/π Sco, 17)
- Heart — Antares (18) — the most dramatic Royal Star
- Sting — Shaula (19)

## Notable astronomical landmarks in the 28 Manazil

1. **Pleiades (Manzil 3, Ath-Thuraya):** Universally important open cluster; the name "Thuraya" is still a common Arabic female name.
2. **Aldebaran (Manzil 4, Ad-Dabaran):** Red giant Royal Star; the name "Aldebaran" entered Western astronomy directly from this Manzil.
3. **Regulus (Manzil 10, Al-Jabhah):** Royal Star; the "little king" at the lion's forehead.
4. **Spica (Manzil 14, As-Simak):** First-magnitude star; "unarmed sky-raiser".
5. **Antares (Manzil 18, Al-Qalb):** Royal Star; red supergiant rival of Mars; heart of the scorpion.
6. **Andromeda Galaxy M31 (Manzil 28, Al-Hut):** Al-Sufi made the FIRST KNOWN WRITTEN OBSERVATION of the Andromeda Galaxy near this Manzil's stars in 964 CE — 967 years before galaxies were understood. This is one of the landmark moments in the history of observational astronomy.
