## Tradition overview

Arctic circumpolar sky-traditions represent some of the most practically precise ethnoastronomy documented anywhere on Earth. Living in environments where the sky is the primary navigation, seasonal, and timing tool — where summer brings weeks of continuous daylight and winter brings prolonged darkness — Arctic peoples developed acute observational astronomy driven by survival necessity. MacDonald (1998) "The Arctic Sky," published by the Royal Ontario Museum and the Nunavut Research Institute (a community-partnered publication), is the authoritative English-language academic source for Inuit astronomy, based on extensive elder consultations. The key rule for this corpus: each Arctic tradition is per-culture. Inuit (Eastern Arctic, Western Arctic, Greenlandic), Sámi, Yupik, and other Arctic peoples are distinct — their sky-traditions are not interchangeable.

## Cross-cultural convergence note

Ursa Major is independently identified as a large hunted herd animal in multiple circumpolar traditions:
- Eastern Arctic Inuit: **Tukturjuit** (Caribou)
- Sámi: **Sarva** (Elk / Moose)
- Some Native American traditions: **Bear** (Iroquois) / **Fisher** (Anishinaabe — per Big Dipper)

This convergence is documented in academic ethnoastronomy. The visual quality of Ursa Major — a large, seven-star figure, circumpolar at high latitudes — makes the 'large animal being hunted' reading repeatedly intuitive for hunting cultures. Each identification is culturally distinct; the pattern is a documented feature of Arctic ethnoastronomy.

## Key academic note: MacDonald (1998) community partnership

The Arctic Sky (1998) was published jointly by the Royal Ontario Museum and the **Nunavut Research Institute** — a community-based Inuit research body. This makes it one of the more ethically grounded publications in this entire corpus: it was produced in genuine partnership with the communities whose knowledge it documents, not through unilateral extraction. This is the sourcing model Protocol B points toward. All Inuit entries in this corpus carry MacDonald (1998) as their primary academic anchor.
