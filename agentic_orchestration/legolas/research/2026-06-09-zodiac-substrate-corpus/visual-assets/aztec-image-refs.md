## THE primary visual reference: Aztec Sun Stone (Piedra del Sol)

The Aztec Sun Stone is the most important Mesoamerican monument for Tonalpohualli visual reference. It is a 3.6-meter basalt disc carved ~1427 CE, currently on display at the Museo Nacional de Antropología, Mexico City (under INAH administration).

**The stone's concentric rings (from center outward):**
1. **Center:** Tonatiuh (Sun God) face with clawed hands grasping hearts, tongue as sacrifice knife
2. **Ring 2:** The four previous world-age suns (4-Jaguar, 4-Wind, 4-Rain, 4-Water)
3. **Ring 3:** The Ollin (movement) glyph — the name of the current (Fifth) Sun — with four directional elements
4. **Ring 4:** The 20 Tonalpohualli day-sign glyphs in sequence — THE canonical glyph reference
5. **Ring 5:** Turquoise and jade symbols (precious materials)
6. **Ring 6:** Sun rays
7. **Outer border:** Two fire serpents (Xiuhcoatl) meeting at the base

**Wikipedia page:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec_sun_stone
**Wikipedia Commons main image:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec_sun_stone#/media/File:Piedra_del_Sol_Calendario_Azteca.jpg

This image (the full Sun Stone) is in the public domain as ancient pre-Columbian art. The Wikipedia Commons upload is high resolution.

**INAH museum photography:** INAH maintains photography rights over images taken within their museums. The Wikipedia Commons image is suitable for research use; for production commercial use, INAH licensing should be verified at https://www.inah.gob.mx/

## Codex Borgia — primary deity imagery source

The Codex Borgia (Codex Yoalli Ehecatl) is a pre-Columbian Aztec tonalamatl (day-count book) of extraordinary richness, currently held by the Vatican Apostolic Library (Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana). It predates the Spanish conquest and provides canonical day-sign deity imagery in full color.

**Wikipedia page:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex_Borgia
**Wikipedia Commons facsimile:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Codex_Borgia
**Content relevant to Tonalpohualli:** Pages 1–8 show the 260-day count with presiding deities; pages 9–13 show the tonalpohualli divided into trecenas; pages 61–70 are particularly rich in deity-day-sign associations

The Codex Borgia is entirely public domain as pre-Columbian art. High-resolution scans are available through the Vatican Library and Wikipedia Commons.

## Codex Borbonicus — colonial-era Aztec day-count book

The Codex Borbonicus is an early colonial-era Aztec codex (c. 1522 CE or shortly after), possibly created by Aztec priests under Spanish oversight. It contains the most complete surviving tonalpohualli with deity imagery.

**Wikipedia page:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex_Borbonicus
**Held by:** Bibliothèque de l'Assemblée nationale, Paris
**Wikipedia Commons:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Codex_Borbonicus

The first 18 pages of the Codex Borbonicus show the 260-day tonalpohualli organized into 20 trecenas (13-day periods), each with the presiding deity painted in full color. This is the most useful source for deity iconography tied to specific trecena positions.

## Xochipilli statue — special high-value reference

The Xochipilli statue (Aztec, c. 1450 CE; National Museum of Anthropology, Mexico City) is one of the most visually extraordinary Aztec sculptures. The flower prince sits in ecstatic trance, covered in stylized flowers, mushrooms, and morning glory vines. This statue presides over Ozomatli (Day 11, Monkey). 

**Wikipedia page:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xochipilli
**Image on Wikipedia Commons:** Search "Xochipilli" on https://commons.wikimedia.org/

INAH photography rights apply to museum photographs of this object.

**Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian:**
- Collections database: https://americanindian.si.edu/explore/collections
- Aztec / Mexica artifacts including calendar objects, deity figurines, obsidian blades (Tecpatl reference)

**INAH — Museo Nacional de Antropología, Mexico City:**
- https://www.mna.inah.gob.mx/
- Houses the Aztec Sun Stone, the Xochipilli statue, and the largest collection of Aztec archaeological artifacts in the world
- INAH digital collections: https://mediateca.inah.gob.mx/

**INAH Templo Mayor Museum:**
- https://www.templomayor.inah.gob.mx/
- Templo Mayor is the excavated Aztec main temple in central Mexico City; the museum houses artifacts from the excavation including deity offerings and sacrifice implements

## Phase 2 visual asset priorities

1. **Sun Stone day-sign ring detail shots** — A high-resolution cropped image of just the 20 day-sign ring on the Sun Stone would provide the most canonical single-image reference for all 20 Aztec glyphs. INAH licensing verification needed for production use.

2. **Codex Borgia pages 1–8** — The tonalpohualli sections with presiding deity imagery in full pre-Columbian color. Wikipedia Commons has scans; resolution suitable for research, may need higher-resolution Vatican Library scans for production.

3. **Codex Borbonicus pages 1–18** — Complete trecena series with deity paintings; Paris Bibliothèque de l'Assemblée nationale holds the original; Wikipedia Commons scans available.

4. **Ollin glyph standalone** — The Ollin four-directional symbol is the single most recognizable Aztec glyph (Sun Stone center). The Wikipedia Commons SVG at https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/5e/Ollin_symbol.svg is production-suitable.

5. **Dual Mayan-Aztec comparative grid** — A side-by-side image showing all 20 Mayan Tzolkin glyphs next to their 20 Aztec Tonalpohualli counterparts would be a unique visual asset. No single Wikipedia image covers this; a composite would need to be generated from the two glyph series.
