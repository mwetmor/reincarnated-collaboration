### 1. Tukturjuit (Caribou) — Ursa Major star map

**Entry:** `inuit-eastern-arctic-001`
**Primary URL:** `https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e7/Ursa_Major_constellation_map.svg/1200px-Ursa_Major_constellation_map.svg.png`
**Wikipedia article:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ursa_Major
**Description:** IAU official constellation map of Ursa Major; shows all stars with the Big Dipper (Plough) asterism prominent. The seven bright stars of the Big Dipper form the core of Tukturjuit (Caribou) in Eastern Arctic Inuit tradition.
**License:** CC-BY-SA (IAU / Wikipedia Commons)
**Key visual information:**
- The Big Dipper (Plough) is the dominant asterism: bowl stars (Dubhe, Merak, Phecda, Megrez) + handle (Alioth, Mizar, Alkaid)
- Circumpolar from latitudes above ~40°N; never sets below the horizon from Arctic latitudes
- The constellation is large: ~25° across; one of the few asterisms that reads as a substantial figure from memory
- Visual note for game use: the Big Dipper's 'bowl' points roughly toward Polaris (the North Star) — following the bowl stars 5× their separation leads to Polaris

**Arctic sky-photography supplement:**
For contextual imagery (caribou + Arctic sky setting):
- Wikipedia Commons search "Caribou Alaska" / "Caribou Arctic tundra" — multiple CC-BY-SA wildlife photographs
- Wikipedia Commons search "Aurora Borealis" — Northern Lights (aurora) photographs for atmospheric context

### 2. Aagjuuk — Aquila star map

**Entry:** `inuit-eastern-arctic-002`
**Primary URL:** `https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c4/Aquila_constellation_map.svg/1200px-Aquila_constellation_map.svg.png`
**Wikipedia article:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquila_(constellation)
**Description:** IAU official constellation map of Aquila; shows Altair (α Aquilae) as the dominant bright star and Tarazed (γ Aquilae) as a nearby orange giant. These two stars = Aagjuuk in Eastern Arctic Inuit tradition.
**License:** CC-BY-SA (IAU / Wikipedia Commons)
**Key visual information:**
- Altair (magnitude 0.77) is prominent; it is one of the three stars of the Summer Triangle (with Vega and Deneb)
- Tarazed (γ Aquilae, magnitude 2.72) is ~2.6° from Altair; a notable orange-giant companion
- Aquila the Eagle: the IAU constellation independently maps to an eagle figure; Western = Eagle; Inuit = the Hunter Stars (Aagjuuk) — different readings of the same region
- From Arctic latitudes (~70°N), Aquila rises only briefly above the southern horizon in summer — making Aagjuuk's brief appearance as a summer-solstice marker especially precise

**Summer solstice / midnight sun visual supplement:**
- Wikipedia Commons search "Arctic midnight sun" — photographs of the Arctic summer sky at midnight
- The Arctic sky at the summer solstice: twilight but never full darkness; Aagjuuk's brief appearance in that twilight is the key calendrical event

### 3. Sakiattiat (Pleiades) — Star cluster photograph

**Entry:** `inuit-circumpolar-001`
**Primary URL:** `https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4e/Pleiades_large.jpg/1200px-Pleiades_large.jpg`
**Wikipedia article:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleiades
**Description:** Photograph of the Pleiades star cluster (M45); seven principal naked-eye stars visible in the blue-white cluster.
**License:** CC-BY-SA (NASA/Wikipedia Commons)
**Key visual information:**
- The Pleiades is one of the most photographed objects in the night sky
- The cluster has associated nebulosity (reflection nebulae); long-exposure photographs show blue-ish wisps around the brighter stars
- From Arctic latitudes the Pleiades is prominent in the winter sky — high overhead in deep winter nights
- The tight clustering of the stars is what drives the "ones that lie in clusters together" (Sakiattiat) reading

**Additional Pleiades images:**
- NASA/ESA Hubble image of the Pleiades: publicly available, NASA images are public domain
- Search Wikipedia Commons "Pleiades NASA" for multiple freely-licensed options

## General visual notes for Inuit / Arctic tradition

**Aurora Borealis (Northern Lights) as contextual backdrop:**
The aurora borealis is strongly associated with Arctic cultures visually. Multiple CC-BY-SA and public-domain aurora photographs are available on Wikipedia Commons (search "Aurora Borealis Iceland" / "Northern Lights Alaska"). Aurora has cultural significance in Inuit tradition (some traditions see the aurora as spirits of the ancestors at play); this is publicly documented but handled with care — use aurora purely as visual atmosphere rather than making specific spiritual claims.

**Arctic night sky:**
The extreme clarity of Arctic skies (low light pollution, low humidity) produces spectacular star photography. Wikipedia Commons search "Arctic night sky" / "Greenland night sky" / "Alaska night sky" for CC-BY-SA options.

**Royal Ontario Museum (ROM) / Nunavut Research Institute:**
MacDonald (1998) "The Arctic Sky" was published with community partnership. The Royal Ontario Museum (ROM) has online collections; some images from their Arctic ethnography collections may be publicly accessible. https://www.rom.on.ca/ — check online collection for freely accessible images related to Inuit material culture.

**Note on Inuit art imagery:**
Traditional Inuit art (soapstone carving, printmaking) is a living and commercialised art form. Use of Inuit art imagery for commercial game purposes requires licensing from the artist or Inuit cultural organizations (Nunavut Arctic College, Dorset Fine Arts, etc.). Do NOT use Inuit art imagery without proper licensing. Star maps and wildlife photographs are the appropriate freely-licensed visual assets for this corpus.
