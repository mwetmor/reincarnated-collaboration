## Summary

The ARPG community's design discourse in 2023–2026 clusters around five recurring tensions: build diversity vs. power standardization, loot generosity vs. loot meaningfulness, live-service season structure vs. player investment permanence, AOE vs. single-target balance, and class fantasy clarity vs. mechanical genericness. The community generally advocates for: broad build viability (no mandatory optimal path), loot that can be found organically (not gated behind trade or AH), seasonal content that adds rather than replaces, AOE and single-target balancing through encounter design rather than flat damage ratios, and class mechanics that create genuinely distinct gameplay — not just different-colored numbers. Anti-patterns are well-documented through recent failures (D4 Season 1 nerfs, Immortal monetization, Wolcen instability) and frequently referenced in genre discussions.

## Findings

### 1. Best Practices — What the Community Considers "Done Right"

#### Build Diversity
The community's most consistently praised design achievement is **Grim Dawn's dual-class system** ("uncontested king of build diversity") — the Devotion constellation system combined with dual masteries creates effectively unlimited character permutations. Last Epoch (1.0, February 2024) is noted for 15 mastery classes across 5 base classes, each with 120+ customizable skills, as a more recent entry that achieves wide-scale build variety.

Community consensus on what produces good build diversity:
- Multiple viable endgame builds per class, not one meta and nine trash tiers
- Both casual and optimized builds able to complete core content
- Distinct *playstyle* differences between builds (not just damage type substitutions)
- No single ability that is so dominant it crowds out all others ("skill tax")

#### Loot Generosity
Since D3/Reaper of Souls normalized smart loot (drops skewed toward your class), the genre consensus moved toward: drops should feel abundant, upgrades should appear frequently enough to maintain engagement, but the ceiling should remain out of reach for regular play. The loot-hunt as primary activity must not be substitutable by trade alone.

From Last Epoch's review: the game was positioned as "remember when ARPGs were about playing the game, not grinding for loot that disappears next season?" — a direct critique of live-service loot cycles where all progress resets.

Community best practice: color-coded rarity visible at a glance; loot filter support as near-mandatory in any game with high drop volumes; item *identity* (named uniques with distinct mechanics > stat-maximization commodities).

#### Season Structure
Dominant model: 10–13 week seasons (PoE leagues established this; D4 adopted it). Community expects:
- A new mechanic per season — not just rebalancing
- Meaningful seasonal rewards that are earnable (not purchasable)
- Economy reset at season start — creates equal footing, prevents power-gap
- Clear end-of-season date communicated in advance

Community criticism: seasons that add content but don't address underlying systems problems are seen as papering over design debt. D4's Season 1 backlash was partly about the disconnect between "new seasonal content" and "the base game's feel-bad issues weren't addressed."

#### Endgame Depth
Community expectation for endgame: multiple distinct activity types (not just the same dungeon at higher tier), each with its own reward profile; meaningful difficulty scaling that tests builds rather than just gating by gear score; optional ultra-hard content for the most invested players (D3's Paragon/Greater Rifts, PoE's Maven/Uber Pinnacle Bosses, Last Epoch's Empowered Monolith).

**Sources:** TechBriefly, Crate Entertainment Forum, Last Epoch impressions (catographer.tokyo), Oreate AI Blog

### 2. Anti-Patterns — What the Community Calls Out Repeatedly

#### Mandatory Grind
Community tolerance for grind has decreased significantly. The line between "satisfying repetition" and "mandatory grind" is described as: does the player *choose* to repeat, or are they *required* to in order to progress at a pace that doesn't feel punishing?

Symptoms of mandatory-grind anti-pattern:
- Specific boss runs required because other content doesn't drop required item types
- Daily/weekly lockouts that cap how much you can progress
- Reputation gates (unlock content by grinding currency with a specific faction repeatedly)

Community sentiment: "players want games that don't waste their time" — the Crate Forum discussion frames the ideal as endless grind being *optional*, not structural.

#### Gear as Gate
When gear score or item level functions as a hard gate to content (rather than a recommendation), the community reads this as: the design is asking players to repeat earlier content *not for fun* but as a prerequisite for new content. This is distinct from "you need to be strong enough to clear this" — it's the *mandatory threshold* framing.

Most cited examples:
- D4 World Tiers gating access to endgame content types
- Lost Ark's Honing system (upgrade failure as mechanic) creating mandatory grind walls before each tier of raid content

#### Bloat
"Bloat" refers to: systems that add complexity without adding meaningful decisions. Examples the community cites:
- Dozens of currency types where most are vendor-trash
- Skill trees with 200 nodes where 180 are "+1% to X"
- Inventory management as primary gameplay — time spent sorting and selling rather than playing
- "Mandatory checklist" quest objectives (collect 30 bear pelts to enter next zone)

PoE acknowledges this tension itself — its currency system is complex, and not all currency types are equally relevant. The community's tolerance for complexity is higher when the complexity produces genuine decisions.

#### Monetization as Mechanic
The hardest consensus anti-pattern: when the monetization model is visibly woven into progression design (energy systems, time gates, loot crates for power). Diablo Immortal is the genre's canonical bad example. The community's litmus test: "would this mechanic exist if there were no monetization?" If the answer is "no" — if the mechanic only makes sense as a purchase prompt — it is considered design corruption.

Live-service ARPG community sentiment (2024): frustration with "bigger live-service games that promise evolving worlds but mostly deliver bug fixes and monetization experiments." Reddit discussions characterized this as player fatigue with the form.

**Sources:** Crate Entertainment Forum, GameSpot (D4), Dexerto (Immortal), TechBriefly

### 3. Loot Design Evolution Across the Genre

| Era | Dominant Model | Exemplar |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-D2 (1996–1999) | Generic stat sticks — items were power numbers | Diablo 1 |
| D2 era (2000–2012) | Named uniques with identity + random rare crafting + rune words | Diablo 2 |
| D3 launch (2012–2014) | AH-optimized gear (stats became commodities again) | Diablo 3 (failure case) |
| D3 post-RoS (2014+) | Smart loot + legendary with unique mechanics | Diablo 3: Reaper of Souls |
| PoE era (2013+) | Currency-as-crafting + socket system + rare self-crafted competition with uniques | Path of Exile |
| Last Epoch (2024) | Hybrid: item factions (trade OR enhanced drops), per-slot crafting system, temporal anchoring | Last Epoch 1.0 |
| D4 (2023+) | Tempered affixes, masterworking, codex of power for aspect stability | Diablo 4 |

**Key evolution markers:**
- **Unique/Legendary identity:** D2 named items had personality (Shako, Enigma); D3 launch legendaries did not; D3 RoS legendaries had unique effects; D4 Aspects codified unique effect sources while Uniques retained named-item identity
- **Player agency in crafting:** PoE moved farthest toward player-directed item crafting; Last Epoch added a deterministic crafting system alongside RNG drops (craft specific stats you want, at cost)
- **Trade vs. self-found tension:** Every major ARPG now addresses this explicitly. Last Epoch's faction system (join trade faction vs. enhanced-solo faction) is the most design-forward solution — the choice is structural, not incidental

**Sources:** Gamedeveloper.com (loot table design: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/defining-loot-tables-in-arpg-game-design), diablo2.io, Maxroll, TheGamer

### 4. AOE vs. Single-Target Balance — Current Discourse

This is among the most technically contested design questions in the ARPG community. There is no consensus formula; the discourse surfaces several competing frameworks.

#### The Core Tension
AOE abilities hit multiple enemies simultaneously. If AOE damage is comparable to single-target damage, there is no mechanical reason to ever use single-target. If AOE damage is sharply penalized per-target, AOE-focused classes are underpowered against elite enemies and bosses.

The ARPG genre historically resolves this by creating distinct *content types* (pack content vs. boss content) where AOE and single-target each have advantages — then balancing the ratio of content types in the game.

#### Documented Design Approaches

**Flat percentage reduction:**
AOE abilities deal a fixed percentage less damage per hit (commonly cited: 80% of single-target, sometimes 60–70%). The "bullet philosophy" articulation: "a bullet can kill a target; many bullets are used if you choose to kill many targets." This is the D4 approach (compound modifiers).

**Resource cost as balance lever:**
AOE abilities cost more mana/energy per cast, creating a sustainable rotation where single-target is economical and AOE is powerful-but-limited. Works when resource management is a meaningful constraint; breaks down when resource restoration is trivial.

**Cooldown as balance lever:**
AOE abilities have longer cooldowns, limiting burst frequency. Viable but creates a rhythm problem: players who invest in AOE are waiting between packs, while single-target players fight constantly.

**Encounter design as the real balancer:**
The community's most sophisticated argument: the ratio of pack content to boss content in the game is the primary AOE/ST balance lever, not the damage numbers. If 80% of encounters are packs and 20% are bosses, AOE-focused builds have 80% of the game "easier." If that ratio inverts (boss-heavy endgame), single-target becomes mandatory.

Community discourse from Last Epoch forum (Balance: AoE and crowd size): encounter design — crowd size per fight, arena geometry — is where the real balance work happens, not the skill tooltip numbers.

**GW2's documented principle:**
A documented ARPG-adjacent design principle (Guild Wars 2 archived forum): "AoE and single-target must have comparable utility when encounter design is accounted for." This means the *game designer's job* is not to make the numbers equal but to ensure each approach has a natural home in the content distribution.

**PoE's approach:**
PoE's endgame mapping is primarily pack-clearing, making AOE builds dominant in map content. Boss encounters (pinnacle bosses, Uber versions) shift the balance toward single-target or builds with both capabilities. The community accepts this as intentional: the challenge of the endgame is partially choosing and building a character that handles *both* content types adequately.

**Sources:** Last Epoch Forum (https://forum.lastepoch.com/t/balance-aoe-and-crowd-size/20210), GW2 forum archive (https://forum-en.gw2archive.eu/forum/game/pvp/AOE-vs-single-target-dmg-Design-Principle-1), Playgama (AOE design guide), RPG Maker Forums (AOE vs ST discussion), Achieving Balance paper (faculty.uca.edu/jbeyerl/Papers/ARPG_Balance.pdf)

### 5. Class Fantasy — What Makes a Class Feel Like Itself

This is the community's most qualitative discourse area. "Class fantasy" describes the feeling that a character class is mechanically expressing a coherent identity — not just numbers but a way of playing.

#### What Creates Class Fantasy (Community Consensus)

**Mechanically distinct engagement pattern:**
A Barbarian who is constantly moving, engaging, and physically striking enemies feels different from a Necromancer who hangs back and lets summons do work — even if their DPS numbers are identical. The *player behavior* must differ, not just the visual skin.

**Identity-locking mechanics:**
Keystones (PoE), Ascendancies (PoE), Mastery choices (Last Epoch) — mechanics that lock in a playstyle identity rather than being additive power increases. "Resolute Technique" (never crit, never miss) changes how you build fundamentally; "+5% crit chance" does not.

**Unique resource or mechanic:**
Classes that manage a unique resource (Druid's Fury, D4 Druid's Spirit, D4 Necromancer's Essence + Bone/Blood/Shadow) have identity anchored to that resource loop. Classes without a unique resource feel generic — they "feel like default characters with themed skin."

**The D3 Sorcerer problem (community framing applied to any game):**
Classes that are "worse version of another class" are the canonical class-fantasy failure. D3's Wizard was often described as "worse than Barbarian in most of its design space." D4's Sorcerer was widely called the weakest at launch specifically because it had a unique resource (Mana) that created constraints without creating commensurate power. Community response: the constraint mechanics (freeze timer, Mana cost) felt punishing, not expressive.

**Sources:** PCGamer, GameSpot, D4 community discourse, PoE forum

### 6. Other Observed Design Discourse Points

**Endgame content that doesn't expire:**
Community frustration with seasonal content that disappears at league end. The "fear of missing out" is considered psychologically manipulative; the "work done feels deleted" creates resentment. Games that archive seasonal mechanics into base game content (PoE's league retention system — some mechanics become permanent base features) receive community credit.

**Transparency in balancing:**
Communities reward developers who show their math. D4's 14,000-word Season 2 patch notes were praised. Season 1's patch notes were criticized not just for the nerfs but for the *opacity* of the reasoning. PoE's manifesto posts (when GGG explains the reasoning behind nerfs) are a community institution.

**Social design vs. solo design:**
There is a significant segment of ARPG players who explicitly prefer solo (or duo) play and resent designs that require grouping for endgame content. Lost Ark's raid-centric endgame was a friction point for Western players expecting a solo-viable ARPG. The community articulation: "if I wanted a party game, I'd play an MMO."

**Sources:** TechBriefly, Crate Forum, GameSpot
